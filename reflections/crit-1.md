# Crit 1 (week 12 makeup)

> **Context:** I wasn't enrolled during Crit 1's original week, so no course-provisioned `comp4020-crit1-<handle>` repository was created for me. While waiting for the convenor's reply, I built a local, standalone attempt at the "Forgotten Web" brief: a three-page GeoCities-style website. The convenor clarified that Week 12 isn't swept, so the tutor marks whatever is presented live in the room. The easiest option was to present my already-deployed final project at [https://comp4020-final-januaraine.fly.dev/](https://comp4020-final-januaraine.fly.dev/) rather than create a separate Crit 1 artefact. This reflection considers both that decision and what I learned from developing and testing my existing project.

**What was the breakthrough that moved the work forward?**

The breakthrough wasn't a coding decision at all — it was asking the convenor directly instead of guessing at a policy that didn't cover my situation. I assumed that missing a crit meant I needed a separate repository and artefact to make up for it, so I spent time building a standalone "Forgotten Web" prototype before confirming whether it was actually required. The convenor's reply reframed the problem: Week 12 isn't swept by the course's automated checks, so the tutor assesses whatever is presented live in the room. The simplest path was therefore to present the final project I'd already deployed rather than continue building a parallel artefact. This taught me to distinguish between what I assume a process requires and what it actually requires, and to clarify uncertainty before committing more time to implementation.

Another important lesson came from letting other people try the final project. Some problems were difficult for me to notice when I was developing and testing the application myself. For example, feedback helped expose lag in the controls and an issue with the light/dark theme toggle on the leaderboard. These were not problems I had fully recognised through my own use of the application. This reminded me that having a feature implemented, or having it appear to work during my own testing, does not necessarily mean it works well for other users. External feedback revealed gaps between how I expected the application to behave and the experience people actually had.

**What did this work change about who I want to be as a software developer?**

It reinforced two things I want to carry into future projects. First, checking an assumption with the person who owns the process is often cheaper than building around a guess. I could have continued extending the standalone prototype without knowing whether it was needed; asking the convenor turned an open-ended problem into a straightforward decision. It also made me more comfortable presenting real, existing work when it meets the purpose, rather than feeling that every situation requires a fresh, purpose-built artefact to demonstrate effort.

Second, I want to become a developer who treats user feedback as an essential part of development, not just something to collect after implementation. When working on my own code, I naturally test it according to my understanding of how it should behave. Other people approach the same application with different expectations and interaction patterns, which can expose issues I have overlooked. The control lag and leaderboard theme-toggle issue made this concrete for me: I cannot rely entirely on my own experience to judge the quality of what I build. In future projects, I want to involve other people earlier, observe how they use the application, and use their feedback to guide debugging and refinement. I want to measure progress not only by whether I have implemented a feature, but also by whether it works reliably and provides a good experience for the people using it.
